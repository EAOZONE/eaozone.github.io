Research · IHMC · May–Aug 2026

GR00T Manipulation

I built a demonstration and training pipeline for humanoid lever turning, adapting existing teleoperation and inverse-kinematics software.

What I built & adapted

  • I extended an existing Isaac Lab environment with lever-turning assets and simulated cameras, integrating the existing teleoperation system for demonstration collection.
  • I wrote a Java converter for real-robot logs and integrated Isaac Teleop’s converter for simulated data. The training dataset contained approximately 20 real and 200 simulated demonstrations.
  • I adapted NVIDIA’s existing Unitree G1 inverse-kinematics code to convert the policy’s predicted wrist poses into arm motion on our robot. The policy used head-camera images and upper-body joint observations.

Testing & results

  • I traced inconsistent arm motion across simulators, including MuJoCo, to a quaternion-convention mismatch. Correcting the action representation made the motion consistent.
  • The trained policy successfully turned the lever in 95% of 1,000 simulation trials with randomized starting positions.
  • The policy was also tested on hardware and successfully turned the lever in one hardware trial.

System architecture

The task was humanoid lever turning for IHMC’s shipboard-manipulation research. I started with an existing robot, teleoperation system, and basic Isaac Lab environment. My work connected demonstration collection, policy training, and robot control.

Demonstration pipeline

Real and simulated data

The real and simulated demonstrations began in different formats. I wrote a Java converter for real-robot logs and integrated Isaac Teleop’s converter for simulated data. The resulting dataset contained approximately 20 real and 200 simulated demonstrations.

In Isaac Lab, I built the lever-turning task assets and added cameras for demonstration collection. Integrating the existing teleoperation system let an operator demonstrate the task in that environment. GR00T was a practical fit for the NVIDIA simulation workflow we were already using.

From wrist poses to arm motion

At runtime, the policy used images from the left and right head cameras together with upper-body joint observations. Its output described wrist poses. Those poses still had to be translated into arm motion on the robot.

I adapted NVIDIA’s inverse-kinematics implementation for the Unitree G1 to our robot. That integration connected the learned policy’s wrist targets to the robot’s arm-control path. The GR00T model, robot platform, and original inverse-kinematics implementation were existing components.

Making actions consistent across simulators

Identical actions initially produced different arm poses in different simulation environments. I compared the resulting motion, including in MuJoCo, and traced the discrepancy to a quaternion-convention mismatch.

Correcting the action representation made the motion consistent across those environments. This mattered before evaluating the learned behavior: a wrist target needed to represent the same pose wherever the policy was tested. The reported evaluation reached 95% success in simulation; hardware testing produced one successful lever-turning trial.
